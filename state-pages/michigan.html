Michigan Knife Laws 2026: What You Can Legally Own and Carry

Michigan has no statewide blade-length limit and repealed its switchblade ban in 2017. The real risk is concealed carry — a dagger, dirk or stiletto out of sight is a felony, and that includes one inside your vehicle.

Researched and published by Michigan Sports Outdoor Last updated 1 September 2026 Primary sources: Michigan Legislature, AKTI

Michigan knife law — key facts

  • Michigan does not set a statewide blade-length limit. No Michigan statute restricts how long a knife blade may be for ordinary carry.
  • Automatic and switchblade knives are legal to own in Michigan. Public Act 96 of 2017 repealed the prohibition, effective 11 October 2017.
  • Carrying a concealed dagger, dirk, stiletto or double-edged non-folding stabbing instrument is a felony under MCL 750.227(1), punishable by up to five years’ imprisonment and a $2,500 fine.
  • The same vehicle rule applies. Possessing those weapons in any vehicle you operate or occupy is covered, whether or not the knife is within reach.
  • Michigan has no knife preemption law. Detroit and Lansing both restrict blades over three inches in public places.
  • Ordinary folding pocket knives are not restricted by Michigan state law and may generally be carried openly or concealed.

Michigan Knife Laws Explained

The six questions Michigan carriers actually ask

Blade length

What Size Knife Can You Legally Carry in Michigan?

Michigan does not limit knife blade length at the state level. No Michigan statute sets a maximum blade size for ordinary carry, so a folding knife of any blade length is generally lawful to carry under state law.

The limit people remember comes from city ordinances, not the state. Detroit and Lansing both restrict carrying knives with blades over three inches in public places. Because Michigan has no knife preemption statute, those local rules stand on their own and apply the moment you cross into the city.

Blade length also appears in MCL 750.226, but only in the context of carrying a dangerous weapon with intent to use it unlawfully — not as a general carry limit. That distinction is explained in full further down this page.

Practical rule: anywhere in Michigan outside Detroit and Lansing, blade length is not your constraint — blade shape is. Keep to single-edged folders and you stay clear of the felony categories.
Source: AKTI — Michigan Knife Laws
Automatics

Are Switchblades and Automatic Knives Legal in Michigan?

Yes. Automatic and switchblade knives are legal to own and possess in Michigan. Public Act 96 of 2017 repealed MCL 750.226a, the statute that had banned them, effective 11 October 2017.

Before that repeal, Michigan prohibited possession of a knife that looked like a pocket knife but opened by a button, spring or other mechanical contrivance. The 2017 act removed that prohibition entirely, putting Michigan among the states that permit automatic knives outright.

The repeal did not create a carry exemption. An automatic knife whose blade is double-edged, or which otherwise meets the description of a dagger or stiletto, remains subject to the concealed-carry felony under MCL 750.227(1). The opening mechanism was legalised; the restricted blade shapes were not.

Source: Public Act 96 of 2017, Michigan Senate Fiscal Agency analysis
Concealed carry

Can You Carry a Concealed Knife in Michigan?

You may carry an ordinary folding pocket knife concealed in Michigan, but carrying a concealed dagger, dirk, stiletto or double-edged non-folding stabbing instrument is a felony under MCL 750.227(1), punishable by up to five years’ imprisonment, a fine of up to $2,500, or both.

Michigan draws its line at the shape and purpose of the blade, not at the opening mechanism or the length. The restricted categories are defined by design:

A dagger is a knife with a straight, double-edged blade made for stabbing. A dirk is a long, straight-bladed stabbing knife. A stiletto is a narrow, needle-pointed blade designed to pierce rather than cut. All three share one trait — they are built primarily to stab, and Michigan treats that as the dividing line.

A conventional single-edged folding knife carried in a pocket does not fall into these categories and is not restricted by Michigan state law.

Source: MCL 750.227(1), Michigan Legislature
Vehicles

Is It Illegal to Keep a Knife in Your Car in Michigan?

Keeping a dagger, dirk, stiletto or double-edged non-folding stabbing instrument in a vehicle you operate or occupy is a felony in Michigan under MCL 750.227(1) — and it applies whether or not the knife is within reach. An ordinary folding pocket knife is not covered by that restriction.

This is the part of Michigan knife law that catches people out. The vehicle provision is not about accessibility. A restricted knife locked in a toolbox in the boot is still covered by the statute, because the offence is possession in the vehicle, not possession within reach.

Practical note: for knives that are lawful to own, some Michigan defence attorneys advise that a knife in the passenger compartment can be treated as concealed, and suggest transporting it in the boot instead. That is practitioner guidance rather than statutory text — if you regularly transport larger fixed blades, confirm it with a licensed Michigan attorney.
Source: MCL 750.227(1), Michigan Legislature
The myth

Is There a 3-Inch Knife Law in Michigan?

There is no general three-inch knife law in Michigan. The three-inch figure appears in MCL 750.226, which applies only when a person carries a dangerous weapon with intent to use it unlawfully against another person — it is not a limit on ordinary carry.

This is the single most repeated error about Michigan knife law, and it appears on forums, in retail listings and in AI-generated guides. The threshold in MCL 750.226 only becomes relevant once unlawful intent has been established. Absent that intent, the statute does not restrict blade length at all.

Where a genuine three-inch rule does exist in Michigan, it is municipal. Detroit and Lansing restrict blades over three inches in public places, and because Michigan has no knife preemption statute, those ordinances are enforceable on their own terms.

Source: MCL 750.226, Michigan Legislature
Local rules

Which Cities in Michigan Restrict Knife Carry?

Detroit and Lansing both restrict carrying knives with blades over three inches in public places. Michigan has no knife preemption statute, so any Michigan city or township may set rules stricter than state law.

Michigan’s preemption statute, MCL 123.1102, bars local governments from regulating pistols, other firearms, pneumatic guns and ammunition. It does not mention knives. A knife preemption bill passed both chambers of the Michigan Legislature in 2021 and was vetoed, so knives remain outside the preemption framework.

The practical consequence: state law tells you what is legal to own, but the city you are standing in tells you what is legal to carry.
